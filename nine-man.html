Nine-Man Racing

Same canoe, same 6 seats paddling at any given moment — but a different race structure and a specific, learnable skill that doesn't come up in standard club-distance or sprint racing: the water change. Most SCORA long-distance races, including the Catalina Crossing, are run this way.

Terms on this page
1

What Nine-Man Racing Is

A Nine-Man crew is 9 total paddlers: 6 paddle in the canoe at any moment, and 3 ride an escort boat, rotating in and out of the canoe throughout the race via water changes — the canoe never stops or slows to a halt for a change; incoming and outgoing paddlers swap in open water while the canoe keeps moving.

This is different from standard OC6 club racing or sprints, where the same 6 paddlers stay in the boat the whole race — no escort boat, no water changes. It's also a different thing entirely from an in-boat side-switch (a "Hut" call, covered on the practice log) — that's about which side of the canoe you paddle on while staying seated, and it happens every 10-15 strokes with nobody leaving the boat. A water change is a completely different event: someone physically exits into the ocean and someone else physically enters, while the canoe is moving at speed.

The escort boat isn't just a taxi for resting paddlers — it usually carries hydration, food, and safety gear too, and its skipper is making real-time navigation and safety decisions the whole race.

2

The Water Change, Step by Step

A water change happens too fast to think through in the moment. The goal of practice is to build it into autopilot — drilled slowly in stages first, reinforced with mental visualization — so in the race it's automatic rather than a moment of panic.

3

Escort Boat Safety & Crew Etiquette

Supporting a Nine-Man race is one of the more hazardous, stressful things a recreational boat skipper will do — tight quarters, multiple canoes, swimmers in the water, constant micro-adjustments. Most escort skippers are volunteers. Reducing their workload and stress is directly the paddlers' own safety margin, not just courtesy.

Your safety, directly

  • Board and position yourselves to keep the boat level side-to-side — uneven weight makes the boat harder to handle, especially at speed or working close to a canoe.
  • Avoid moving around the boat unnecessarily; a stray swell plus a boat maneuver can throw you off balance.
  • Don't trust "non-skid" surfaces as fully secure footing.
  • Never jump into the water until the skipper gives the okay — he may still be making small position adjustments.
  • Never approach the boat while the engine is running during a change. Approach the boarding ladder/swim step from the side away from the propeller.
  • Don't step off the boat at the dock until the skipper says so.
  • Don't use Bimini poles for support getting on/off or standing.
  • Don't sit on the bow of a cuddy-cabin boat while underway — sliding off means real risk of being run over.
Per USCG accident data, "improper loading" and "standing/sitting on the gunwale, transom, bow, or seatback" carry the two highest fatality rates of any listed accident cause — and both are entirely within paddlers'/crew's own control, unlike most other listed causes.

Basic consideration, to keep good skippers coming back

  • Bring only what you need aboard — these boats have little spare room.
  • Be mindful of where you step, especially getting on/off (docks are dirty; that dirt goes straight onto the boat).
  • Arrive on time — meaning at or before the canoe launches.
  • For longer events, prefer a few small coolers over one big one — easier to tuck away.
  • Skip the foul language; you don't know who it'll bother.
  • Keep gear together rather than loose on deck; pack out your own trash and offer to take any other trash you see.

Pre-race coordination (steersperson + coach)

  • Meet with your escort skipper before the race to agree on signals — when paddlers should drop into the water, where the escort should sit relative to the canoe, and any other preferences.
  • Give clear instructions but never pressure a skipper to push past the limits of the rules or their own equipment.

Team culture

  • Treat the escort skipper as an essential team member, not a rideshare. A little recognition goes a long way toward getting a good skipper back next year.
  • If a skipper genuinely disregards safety, report it to club leadership or race officials — don't just quietly avoid them next time.
