Everything a Paddler Should Know

Two tracks: what a brand-new paddler needs before their first real practice, and what veterans keep sharp on a recurring basis. Quick Reference

    A

    History & Tradition

    The sport carries centuries of Native Hawaiian history and belief. Knowing where it comes from changes how you sit in the boat.

    Outrigger canoes are how the Pacific was settled in the first place. Polynesian voyagers navigated by star, wind, and wave to reach the Hawaiian islands roughly 700 to 1,000 years ago, using double-hulled ocean-going wa'a built for open crossings, not just coastal use. Smaller outrigger canoes that followed became the everyday vessel of the islands — for fishing, trade between islands, travel, and eventually racing, when fishermen began challenging each other to be first back to shore.

    The sport nearly didn't survive contact with the West. Missionary influence led Ka'ahumanu to ban canoe racing around 1830, and it stayed dormant for decades. King David Kalakaua revived it in 1875 by declaring his own birthday the official annual regatta day, and outrigger clubs have carried the tradition forward since. The 1976 voyage of the Hōkūle'a — sailing from Hawai'i to Tahiti using only traditional wayfinding, no instruments — proved to the world that the original crossings were no accident, and sparked the cultural revival the sport is still riding today.

    The canoe is a crewmate, not equipment. In Hawaiian tradition, a canoe is considered a living entity with its own mana — spiritual power. Some clubs describe an OC6 crew as seven, not six: the paddlers, plus the canoe itself. That's not a figure of speech to the people who carry the tradition — it's why a canoe is blessed when it's built, cared for like family, and never treated as disposable gear.

    That belief shows up in the physical design, too. Look at the stern of a traditional canoe and you'll often find a small, otherwise unexplained flat spot called the momoa — by tradition, the seat for the canoe's own protective spirit. The story behind it goes back roughly 700 years: a priest left behind on a departing voyage leapt from a cliff and transformed into a spirit mid-flight so he could ride in that one small space. Every canoe built with a momoa since carries a version of that same idea — that the canoe watches over its crew as much as the crew relies on the canoe.

    Customs worth knowing — consistent across outrigger clubs from Hawai'i to Oregon to Australia:

    Never step over the canoe
    Walk around the hull and ama instead of stepping over them, on land or at the dock. It's considered disrespectful and bad luck. If you genuinely have no other way through, the tradition is to acknowledge it — a quick apology to the canoe isn't overkill, it's the custom.
    Never stand in the canoe
    Practical as much as spiritual — it's how canoes flip — but also treated as a mark of disrespect.
    No swearing in the canoe
    The canoe is present for everything that happens in it. Frustration goes to the dock, not the boat — the same principle behind keeping negative calls out of the boat in general.
    Point the bow to the sea
    When storing a canoe, point the bow toward the water where possible — a sign of respect, with roots in war canoes needing a fast launch.
    Blessing a canoe
    New canoes are traditionally blessed before their first time on the water, asking for safe passage for the canoe and everyone who rides in it.
    Care for it like family
    Clean and wipe down the canoe after every outing, the same way you'd look after a boat that's kept you and your crew safe — because that's exactly what it did.

    This tradition belongs to Native Hawaiian culture — outrigger clubs around the world, including the ones this whole practice log draws from, carry it forward as a living part of the sport, not a decoration. For the fuller version — history, language, and the stories behind customs like momoa — Lōkahi Canoe Club's culture page (linked in the resource list below) is worth reading in full.
